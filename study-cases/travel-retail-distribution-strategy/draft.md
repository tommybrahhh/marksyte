## How a Travel Retail KAM team can turn distribution ambition into a customer, portfolio and investment strategy

## Executive summary

An international FMCG brand had a clear Travel Retail ambition: secure more listings, enter more airports and turn the channel into a visible source of growth.

The commercial team initially treated distribution as the primary objective. More doors appeared to mean more passengers, more sales and more negotiating weight. But the opportunity pipeline mixed very different airports, operators, store formats, passenger profiles and commercial conditions. The same portfolio was being presented too widely, customer proposals lacked location-specific evidence, and the cost of supporting each listing was not consistently included in the decision.

The issue was not a shortage of distribution opportunities. It was the absence of a shared definition of **good distribution**.

Marksyte's role in a case like this is to help the KAM team answer four commercial questions:

1. Which accounts and locations are genuinely attractive?
2. Where does the brand have a credible right to win?
3. Which products, packs and activation model fit each opportunity?
4. What evidence should trigger a pilot, wider rollout, renegotiation or exit?

The resulting approach replaces a door-count target with a distribution quality model. It gives KAMs a sharper sell-in story, finance a clearer view of account economics, and marketing and supply teams a more focused set of priorities.

## Situation: traffic growth reopened the distribution conversation

Travel demand gives brands a strong reason to revisit the channel. ACI World reports that global airports handled an estimated **9.8 billion passengers in 2025**, 3.7% more than in 2024 and 6.5% above 2019. International passenger traffic reached approximately **4.0 billion**, growing 6.1% year on year. The scale and momentum are real. ([ACI World, 2026](https://aci.aero/resources/busiest-airports-in-the-world/))

But passenger growth does not translate mechanically into retail growth. ACI's analysis of airport economics shows that global non-aeronautical revenue per passenger fell from **USD 8.61 in 2017 to USD 7.57 in 2024**. It also reports that passenger traffic stood around 4% above 2019 while non-aeronautical revenue remained around 9% below it. Retail concessions are still commercially critical—23.1% of global non-aeronautical income and the largest source in every region except North America—but the productivity challenge is visible. ([ACI World Insights, 2026](https://blog.aci.aero/airport-economics/airport-non-aeronautical-revenues-commercial-strategy/))

That gap matters to a supplier KAM. Airport operators and retailers are not buying access to passenger volume alone. They are trying to increase spend, conversion, margin, operational productivity and the quality of the traveller experience.

The brand in this composite case already had:

- Strong awareness in selected domestic markets
- A portfolio broad enough to offer multiple formats and price points
- Some Travel Retail listings, but uneven performance by operator and location
- Interest from new markets and customers
- A global ambition that was moving faster than the available account evidence

The original management question was:

> **How quickly can we expand distribution?**

The commercially useful question became:

> **Where can this brand create enough shopper, customer and financial value to earn—and retain—distribution?**

## The problems: distribution was growing faster than the commercial logic

### 1. Opportunity size was being confused with opportunity quality

Large passenger numbers made major airports look automatically attractive. Yet airport totals said little about the passengers passing the relevant terminal, their nationalities and missions, the retailer's category strategy, store access, dwell time, price expectations or the brand's likely conversion.

A high-traffic airport could still be a weak brand opportunity. A smaller, better-matched location could generate stronger rate of sale, margin and learning.

### 2. The same portfolio was being sold into different retail environments

Travel Retail is not one homogeneous channel. Gebr. Heinemann describes operations spanning airports, borders, cruises and ferries, inflight, downtown, diplomatic and military locations. It also reports that distribution represents 33% of group turnover, demonstrating that distribution itself covers a substantial and operationally varied business. ([Gebr. Heinemann Annual Report 2025](https://reports.gebr-heinemann.de/2025/fileadmin/statics/pdfs/facts_and_figures/gh-25-annual_report_corporateprofile.pdf))

The brand nevertheless approached many opportunities with a standard range. Core products, travel-exclusive packs, gifts, immediate-consumption formats and innovations were not assigned clear jobs by account or location.

For the buyer, this created assortment duplication and uncertain space productivity. For the KAM, it weakened the rationale behind every SKU request.

### 3. Distribution targets rewarded openings, not sustainable performance

The sales dashboard tracked listings and sell-in. It was less consistent on:

- Rate of sale per point of distribution
- Net margin after customer terms and activation
- Availability and replenishment
- Incrementality versus substitution within the range
- Promotional dependence
- Repeat orders after launch
- Performance against the retailer's category benchmark

This meant a new door could count as a success before shopper demand or account economics had been proven.

### 4. Customer proposals were brand-led rather than customer-specific

The sell-in story explained why the brand was attractive globally. It did not always explain why a particular operator should allocate space in a particular location.

That distinction matters in a concentrated but highly localised customer landscape. Lagardère Travel Retail, for example, reports that its Travel Essentials network alone includes **2,719 stores**, more than **240 retail names**, more than **220 airports** and over **580 rail locations** across 35 countries. One global customer can therefore contain many distinct buying contexts. ([Lagardère Universal Registration Document 2025](https://www.lagardere.com/wp-content/uploads/2026/04/260417_lag_deu_gb.pdf))

### 5. The support cost of distribution was fragmented across functions

Listing fees, margin structure, promotional funding, fixtures, sampling, staff training, travel-exclusive packaging, forecasting complexity and minimum production quantities were evaluated in different places—or too late.

The KAM owned the revenue target but did not always have one transparent view of the investment and execution required to make the listing productive.

## The reasons: why apparently attractive distribution underperformed

### Reason 1: passenger volume was used as a substitute for shopper understanding

Passengers are not automatically shoppers, and shoppers are not equally valuable for every category or brand. The relevant demand pool changes by nationality, route, terminal, travel purpose, party composition, trip duration, departure time and purchase mission.

Retailers themselves describe a more granular model. Avolta says it monitors traveller profiles and expectations using operational information, field surveys, social monitoring and external research. It also conducts performance reviews at airport, location and outlet level to adjust format, assortment and service. ([Avolta Annual Report 2025—Strategy](https://annualreport.avoltaworld.com/2025/en/strategy))

The implication for KAMs is direct: a global brand story is not sufficient evidence for a local listing.

### Reason 2: distribution fit had not been separated from brand fit

A brand may be relevant to travellers but poorly suited to a particular route to market. Pack size, shelf life, customs status, storage, merchandising requirements, replenishment frequency, price ladder and staff explanation can all change the economics.

The question is not only “Will travellers want it?” It is also “Can this customer sell it productively in this environment?”

### Reason 3: SKU roles were unclear

Without explicit roles, every product competed for the same space and investment. A more disciplined Travel Retail portfolio assigns jobs such as:

- **Traffic:** recognisable products that make the fixture easy to understand
- **Conversion:** accessible packs or propositions that remove purchase barriers
- **Margin:** premium or differentiated products that strengthen value creation
- **Gift:** formats designed around portability, presentation and recipient value
- **Destination:** products with local or route relevance
- **Innovation:** tightly controlled tests that generate learning

This is also why localisation is commercially meaningful rather than cosmetic. In its Norway business, Heinemann describes new, locally sourced categories and reports that Norwegian fish oil became the leading newly introduced product, particularly among Chinese travellers. The example shows how regulation, local identity and passenger demand can combine to create a location-specific assortment opportunity. ([Gebr. Heinemann, Norway case, 2025](https://reports.gebr-heinemann.de/2025/one-vision/norway-partnership-and-performance-in-a-regulated-landscape))

### Reason 4: account economics stopped at gross sales

The business case did not consistently combine:

**Expected sell-out**  
minus customer margin and commercial terms  
minus activation and fixture costs  
minus supply and complexity costs  
minus risk allowances for markdown, expiry or delisting  
= **contribution after support**

Without this view, low-quality distribution could absorb disproportionate management time and investment while appearing positive in a topline report.

### Reason 5: commercial teams lacked shared decision rules

Sales, marketing, finance and supply were each applying reasonable but different criteria. Sales valued strategic relationships and revenue; marketing valued visibility; finance valued contribution; supply valued predictability and scale.

The organisation did not lack expertise. It lacked a common model for resolving the trade-offs.

## Support from Marksyte: build a distribution system the KAM team can use

Marksyte's role is not to produce another market report. It is to convert fragmented evidence into account choices, customer arguments and execution rules.

### Workstream 1: establish the distribution baseline

Marksyte combines available sell-in, sell-out, margin, distribution, inventory, promotion and activation data into a single account-location-SKU view.

The baseline answers:

- Where is the brand listed today?
- Which listings are productive, marginal or value-destructive?
- Where are sales explained by genuine rate of sale rather than door count?
- Which SKUs add incremental value?
- Where are availability, execution or data gaps distorting the picture?

**KAM output:** an account performance map that can be used in internal reviews and customer preparation.

### Workstream 2: score opportunities on attractiveness and ability to win

Each opportunity is evaluated across two dimensions.

#### Market and customer attractiveness

- Relevant passenger pool and traffic trajectory
- Category size and growth
- Store and terminal access
- Customer strategy and format fit
- Margin pool and expected productivity
- Strategic relevance beyond immediate revenue

#### Brand ability to win

- Awareness and demand among relevant travellers
- Occasion and mission fit
- Portfolio and price architecture
- Operational and regulatory fit
- Customer relationship and execution capability
- Investment required to reach a viable rate of sale

The output is not a league table based on traffic. It is a choice among four actions:

1. **Scale:** attractive opportunity, proven ability to win
2. **Pilot:** attractive opportunity, incomplete proof
3. **Repair:** existing access, weak productivity or economics
4. **Deprioritise:** insufficient value relative to effort and investment

**KAM output:** a defensible account pipeline with a reason for every priority.

### Workstream 3: define the portfolio by customer and mission

Marksyte assigns each SKU a commercial role and creates recommended range scenarios by format, passenger profile and customer type.

The work tests:

- Core versus extended range
- Travel-exclusive versus domestic packs
- Entry, mainstream and premium price points
- Gift versus immediate-consumption missions
- Incrementality and cannibalisation
- Operational complexity and minimum viable scale

**KAM output:** a customer-specific range rationale rather than a generic product catalogue.

### Workstream 4: build the full account economics

For priority opportunities, Marksyte develops a scenario model linking:

- Passenger and shopper assumptions
- Conversion and units per transaction
- Expected rate of sale
- Customer margin and terms
- Promotional and activation support
- Fixture, logistics and supply costs
- Base, upside and downside cases
- Payback and exit thresholds

This gives the KAM a negotiation envelope: what the business can invest, what performance is required, and which terms would make the opportunity unattractive.

**KAM output:** clearer give-get logic before the buyer meeting.

### Workstream 5: create the customer sell-in story

The final narrative is built around the retailer's commercial problem:

1. The relevant traveller or shopper opportunity
2. The unmet category or mission need
3. The role the brand and selected SKUs can play
4. The expected effect on conversion, basket, margin or experience
5. The activation and operational plan
6. The proposed test and measurement framework

This reflects the way leading retailers describe value creation. Avolta explicitly links shared data and coordinated product innovation with suppliers to improved relevance and conversion. ([Avolta Annual Report 2025—Strategy PDF](https://annualreport.avoltaworld.com/2025/downloads/en/Avolta%20Strategy.pdf))

**KAM output:** a buyer-ready deck, negotiation evidence and objection-handling logic.

### Workstream 6: install a test-learn-scale cadence

Every pilot starts with agreed success criteria:

- Numeric and weighted distribution
- On-shelf availability
- Rate of sale
- Conversion where data is available
- Gross and contribution margin
- Incremental sales versus the existing range
- Promotional uplift and post-promotion baseline
- Repeat order and replenishment quality
- Execution compliance

The review leads to one of four decisions: scale, adapt, hold or exit.

**KAM output:** evidence for the next listing conversation and protection against open-ended pilots.

## Commercial outcome: what changes for the business

Because this is a composite case, no client result is claimed. In a live engagement, outcomes should be reported using verified measures such as listings secured, rate-of-sale change, margin improvement, investment avoided or time saved.

The immediate value of the work is a better quality of decision:

- KAMs focus on the accounts and locations with the strongest value potential.
- Buyers receive a proposal tied to their passengers, format and category economics.
- The portfolio becomes easier to understand, list and execute.
- Finance can see the true contribution and downside of each opportunity.
- Marketing invests behind a defined shopper mission and commercial objective.
- Supply teams can plan against a prioritised pipeline and fewer low-scale variants.

## The lesson for Travel Retail KAMs

The growth of global travel makes distribution opportunity larger. It does not make every door equally valuable.

For KAMs, the practical lessons are:

1. **Do not sell passenger volume as shopper demand.** Translate traffic into the relevant demand pool for the category and location.
2. **Give every SKU a job.** Space is easier to defend when each product contributes a distinct commercial role.
3. **Model contribution after support.** A listing is not profitable simply because it generates sell-in.
4. **Make the proposal customer-specific.** Global brand strength must be converted into local category value.
5. **Agree the scale trigger before the pilot.** Distribution should expand when shopper response, execution and economics have been proven together.

The strongest distribution strategy is therefore not the one that produces the longest customer list. It is the one that helps the brand and retailer identify where each additional door can create sustainable value.
